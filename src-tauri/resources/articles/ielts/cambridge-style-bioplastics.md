Conventional plastics, made from refined petroleum, are among the most useful materials ever invented. They are cheap, light, strong, and almost endlessly versatile. They are also, as the world has slowly come to realise, almost endlessly persistent. A plastic shopping bag thrown away today may still be recognisable a century from now, and the broken-down fragments of older plastic items are now found in soils, rivers, and oceans across the entire planet. The search for replacement materials that share the useful properties of conventional plastics without their long environmental afterlife has become one of the most active fields of modern materials research.

The plastics now generally called "bioplastics" fall into two quite different categories that are often confused with each other. The first category consists of plastics made from biological raw materials, such as maize starch or sugar cane, rather than from oil. These are described as bio-based. The second category consists of plastics that, by virtue of their chemical structure, can be broken down by living organisms into simple natural compounds such as carbon dioxide and water. These are described as biodegradable. A plastic may be bio-based but not biodegradable; it may be biodegradable but not bio-based; and the most attractive materials, of course, are those that are both.

The best-known bio-based plastic is polylactic acid, or PLA, which is produced by fermenting plant sugars into lactic acid and then linking the resulting molecules together into long chains. PLA can be processed using much of the same machinery already used for conventional plastics, and it is now widely used for food packaging, disposable cups, and the filament of small three-dimensional printers. It does break down, but only under fairly specific conditions: a hot, moist environment with the right population of microbes, such as the inside of an industrial composting facility. In an ordinary garden compost heap, and even more so on the open ground or at the bottom of the sea, PLA can persist for many years.

A second important family of bioplastics, called polyhydroxyalkanoates or PHAs, behaves very differently. These materials are produced not by chemical synthesis but directly by bacteria, which manufacture the polymer inside their own cells as a way of storing energy, in much the same way that humans store fat. The bacteria can then be harvested and the polymer extracted from them. PHAs are genuinely biodegradable in a wide range of natural environments, including soil and seawater, which makes them particularly attractive for applications in which used material is likely to escape into the wider environment. The drawback is cost: producing plastic from bacteria is still considerably more expensive than producing it from oil, and PHAs remain a small fraction of the overall plastics market.

A further set of difficulties is less technical than logistical. Many compostable plastics look almost identical to conventional ones and cannot easily be distinguished by a member of the public putting out the rubbish. When compostable items are placed in conventional recycling streams, they contaminate the recovered plastic and may render whole batches unusable. When conventional items are placed in composting streams, they survive the process and have to be sorted out by hand. Until disposal systems are upgraded to deal with the new materials reliably, even an excellent biodegradable plastic may end up in the wrong place and undo much of the benefit it was designed to provide.

For all these problems, the long-term prospects for bioplastics look reasonably good. The cost of the materials has been falling steadily as production scales up, the range of available chemistries has broadened, and many large manufacturers have committed themselves to gradual replacement of petroleum-based packaging. None of this will solve the problem of plastic waste on its own. A genuine solution will also require deeper changes in the way products are designed, used, and collected. But bioplastics, used thoughtfully alongside reduced consumption and better recycling, are likely to be part of the answer rather than a distraction from it.
