By the end of the nineteenth century, the great industrial cities of Britain had become almost unmanageable. London, Manchester, and Birmingham had swollen far beyond the limits anyone had imagined a few decades earlier, and the consequences were everywhere apparent: crowded slums, polluted air, poor sanitation, and stretches of countryside swallowed up by suburbs that nobody had planned. Reformers proposed many remedies, but the most influential, and in the long run the most consequential, was a small book published in 1898 by an English clerk named Ebenezer Howard, originally titled *Tomorrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform*.

Howard's idea was deceptively simple. The advantages of city and countryside, he argued, could be combined in a new kind of settlement, neither suburb nor country town, that he called a garden city. Each garden city would have a planned population of around thirty thousand people, surrounded by a permanent belt of farmland that would supply it with food and prevent further outward growth. The town itself would be designed from the start with broad streets, generous parks, and a clear separation between residential districts and industrial areas. Crucially, the land beneath the town and its surrounding belt would not be owned by individual speculators but held in common by the residents, so that any increase in its value would benefit the whole community.

To turn theory into practice, Howard and a small group of supporters formed a company and, in 1903, began building the first garden city at Letchworth, about fifty kilometres north of London. The architects Raymond Unwin and Barry Parker laid out the streets in gentle curves that followed the existing landscape, allowed generous gardens to each house, and grouped public buildings around small squares. A second garden city, Welwyn, was begun in 1919. Neither town grew as quickly as its founders had hoped—both took several decades to fill out—but each survived as a coherent settlement and avoided the worst features of the unplanned suburbs that surrounded London during the same period.

The international influence of the garden city movement was out of all proportion to the modest size of these early examples. Howard's book was translated into many languages, and from the 1900s onwards, towns described as garden cities, or as garden suburbs, were built in Germany, France, Russia, Japan, the United States, and Australia. In most cases, only a part of Howard's original idea was adopted. Designers borrowed the curving streets, the generous open spaces, and the well-laid-out housing, but few of the imitators preserved the principle of common land ownership, and even fewer maintained the permanent agricultural belt. What were built were essentially attractive low-density suburbs, dependent on the older central city for employment and services.

After the Second World War, the garden city idea returned to its country of origin in a new form. The British government's New Towns Act of 1946 authorised a programme of completely new settlements to absorb population from overcrowded London and other major cities. The eight new towns built around London in the years that followed, beginning with Stevenage in 1946, were in many ways modern descendants of Letchworth: planned from the start, sized in advance, separated by green belts from the cities they served, and supplied with their own employment and shopping centres. Similar programmes were launched in France, the Netherlands, and Scandinavia, with broadly comparable results.

In the twenty-first century, the garden city has come back into fashion once again, in the context of debates about sustainable urban growth. Critics point out that the original garden cities, low-density and dependent on the motor car for journeys outside the town centre, are not in fact especially environmentally efficient. Defenders reply that the principle—planning new settlements from the beginning, surrounding them with protected open land, and giving residents a stake in the increasing value of the land beneath them—remains as relevant as ever, and that the failures of more recent suburban sprawl illustrate exactly the problems Howard set out to solve. Whether the latest revival produces results as durable as Letchworth and Welwyn remains to be seen.
