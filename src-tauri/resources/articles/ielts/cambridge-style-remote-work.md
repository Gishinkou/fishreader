For most of the twentieth century, the typical office worker followed a routine that would have been recognisable to a clerk of the eighteen-eighties: a journey from home to a fixed place of work, a day spent at a desk among colleagues, and a return journey in the evening. The office building itself, and the rituals organised around it, were so taken for granted that they were rarely examined. A series of changes over the past two decades, and especially the abrupt experiment forced on millions of workers by the global health crisis of 2020, has shaken this arrangement more thoroughly than any earlier wave of management theory ever managed to do.

The technical conditions for working away from the office have been in place for longer than is sometimes recognised. Reliable home internet connections, portable computers, and the cluster of software tools needed to share files, hold meetings, and track tasks had all matured well before the pandemic. What was missing was a willingness on the part of senior managers to trust that work would actually get done in their absence. Surveys carried out in the years before 2020 consistently showed that the great majority of office workers, if asked, said they would prefer to work from home at least part of the week; surveys of their managers consistently showed deep reluctance to allow it.

The events of 2020 settled the practical question abruptly. Faced with public-health restrictions, even the most traditional employers sent their staff home, often within a few days, and discovered that most kinds of office work could continue with surprisingly little loss of output. Many companies that had previously rejected requests to work from home as impractical found themselves, almost overnight, operating as fully distributed organisations. By the time restrictions were lifted, a new set of expectations had taken hold among employees, and a complete return to the pre-pandemic arrangement turned out to be politically impossible in many sectors. Some kind of hybrid working pattern, in which staff divided the week between home and office, became the new normal in most large organisations.

The benefits of remote work are real and reasonably well documented. Employees save the time and cost of a daily commute, often gain greater control over their own working hours, and report higher satisfaction with the balance between work and the rest of life. Employers gain access to a wider labour pool, since recruitment is no longer tied to the cities where their offices happen to be located, and they can often reduce the amount of expensive office space they need to maintain. In environmental terms, the reduction in commuter traffic during the most intensive phase of remote work was large enough to be visible in air-quality measurements over major cities.

The drawbacks are equally real and have taken somewhat longer to come into focus. New employees, in particular, report difficulty in learning the unwritten rules of an organisation when they meet their colleagues only on a screen. Informal exchanges in corridors and over coffee, which research has long identified as an important channel for the spread of ideas across an organisation, are difficult to replicate online. Some managers worry, with limited but growing evidence, that purely remote teams generate fewer of the surprising connections from which innovation often springs. Workers themselves report a tendency for the working day to expand into the evening, since the visible boundary provided by leaving the office is no longer there.

The likely long-term outcome is neither the complete return to the old office nor the disappearance of physical workplaces altogether. Most organisations that have studied the question seriously appear to be settling on a model in which staff come to a shared workplace for a small number of days each week, with the rest of the time spent at home or in flexible local offices. The office itself is being redesigned around what people now need it for—meetings, collaboration, training, and the social cohesion of the team—rather than around rows of identical desks. After more than a century of remarkable stability, the basic geography of office work is being rearranged in ways whose full consequences will not be clear for years to come.
