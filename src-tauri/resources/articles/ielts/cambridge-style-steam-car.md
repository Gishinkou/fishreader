At the dawn of the twentieth century, anyone trying to predict the future of road transport faced a genuinely open question. Three rival technologies—the petrol engine, the electric motor, and the steam engine—were all in serious commercial development, and each had passionate supporters. A century later, the petrol engine has so completely dominated personal transport that it is easy to forget that the outcome was once far from obvious. In 1900, in fact, steam cars outsold both their petrol and electric competitors in the United States, and many engineers regarded steam as the only mature and reliable option.

The technology of the steam car was a direct descendant of the steam locomotive. A small coal- or kerosene-fired burner heated water in a compact boiler to produce high-pressure steam, which was then admitted to a piston engine that drove the wheels. Compared with the petrol engines of the period, the steam engine had several genuine advantages. It produced enormous torque from a standstill, so steam cars did not need a complicated gearbox; the driver simply opened a valve and the car moved off smoothly. The engines themselves were mechanically simple, with few moving parts, and they ran almost silently—a striking contrast to the noisy, vibrating petrol vehicles of the time. They were also remarkably flexible in their choice of fuel, accepting almost any flammable liquid.

The most famous American manufacturer of steam cars was the firm founded by the Stanley brothers in Massachusetts. Their early models, light and surprisingly fast, attracted wealthy customers who wanted a fashionable alternative to the horse and carriage. In 1906 a Stanley steamer set a world land-speed record of just over two hundred kilometres per hour on the beach at Daytona, a result that no petrol car of the period could match. Other manufacturers, in Britain and France as well as in the United States, produced steam cars of their own, and in some cities steam-powered buses and delivery vans were a common sight well into the 1920s.

For all these advantages, the steam car suffered from one fundamental drawback: the long delay between starting the burner and having a usable supply of steam. In the earliest models, a driver might need to wait twenty or thirty minutes for the boiler to come up to pressure on a cold morning. Engineers worked hard to reduce this figure—later Stanley models could be on the road in three or four minutes—but no amount of refinement could match the convenience of simply turning a key. Steam cars also consumed water at a considerable rate, and in the days before sealed, condensing boilers became widely available, drivers had to refill the tank at frequent intervals.

The decisive blow came not from the steam car's own weaknesses but from rapid improvements in its competitors. In 1912, the American engineer Charles Kettering introduced the electric starter motor for petrol engines. Before this innovation, petrol cars had to be started by hand-cranking, a process that was difficult, dangerous, and a particular obstacle for women drivers. With a press-button start, petrol cars suddenly became as easy to operate as steam cars, while retaining the advantages of an instantly available power supply and a long range between refuels. At the same moment, mass-production techniques pioneered by Henry Ford were driving down the price of petrol cars far faster than the price of their hand-built steam rivals.

By the late 1920s, steam-car production in the United States had effectively ended. A few enthusiasts continued to experiment with the technology, and from time to time engineers have proposed reviving it, particularly during periods of high oil prices or concern about exhaust pollution. None of these efforts have come close to commercial success. The steam car remains a striking example of how a mature, well-engineered technology can lose out, not because of any single failure, but because a younger rival improves faster than it does.
