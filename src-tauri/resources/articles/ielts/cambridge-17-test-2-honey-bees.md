Honey bees are remarkable for their highly organized social life, and few aspects of their behavior have fascinated scientists more than the way individual bees share information with the rest of the colony. The pioneering work of the Austrian biologist Karl von Frisch in the early twentieth century revealed that returning foragers communicate the location of food sources through a series of stylized movements known as the "waggle dance."

When a worker bee discovers a rich source of nectar or pollen, she returns to the hive and begins to perform a dance on the vertical surface of the honeycomb. The dance consists of a short straight run, during which the bee waggles her abdomen from side to side, followed by a semicircular return to the starting point. After completing the dance once, she repeats it, alternating the direction of the semicircle.

The information conveyed in the dance is surprisingly precise. The direction of the straight, waggling section indicates the angle of the food source relative to the sun. The duration of the waggling tells the other bees how far away the source is: the longer the run, the greater the distance. Surrounding workers follow the dancer closely, picking up the scent of the flowers from her body and the sound vibrations produced by her wings.

Researchers have since shown that the dance is not the only channel of communication available to bees. Chemical signals, called pheromones, play a vital role in many aspects of colony life, from coordinating defense against intruders to maintaining the unique identity of each hive.

Modern studies, often using high-speed cameras and computer analysis, have refined our understanding of how reliably the dance transmits information. Although individual bees sometimes make small errors, the colony as a whole behaves as if it possesses a remarkably accurate map of its surroundings.

The communication system of honey bees is now widely studied not only by biologists, but also by computer scientists interested in how simple rules followed by individuals can give rise to complex collective behavior.
