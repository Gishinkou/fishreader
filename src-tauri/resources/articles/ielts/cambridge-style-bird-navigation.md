Every autumn, billions of birds leave the temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere and travel south to wintering grounds that may be thousands of kilometres away. In spring, the same individuals return, often to the very tree or hedgerow where they bred the previous year. How birds achieve this feat of long-distance navigation, frequently at night and across stretches of open ocean where no landmarks are visible, has been a subject of patient research for more than a century, and the picture that has emerged is more elaborate than anyone originally suspected.

The earliest serious experiments on bird navigation, conducted in the middle of the twentieth century, established that the sun and the stars play a central role. Birds tested in circular cages on clear days will spontaneously hop in the direction of their normal migratory route, but if the apparent position of the sun is shifted by means of mirrors, their preferred direction shifts by exactly the same angle. To use the sun as a compass, however, the bird must compensate for its movement across the sky during the course of a day, which means it must possess an internal clock. Experiments in which birds' biological clocks were artificially reset confirmed that this is exactly what happens. Other studies, carried out in planetariums, showed that nocturnal migrants such as warblers can orient themselves by the stars, and in particular by the pattern of constellations near the celestial pole.

In the 1960s, researchers discovered that birds could still find the right direction even when the sky was completely covered with cloud, which suggested the existence of a second compass. A series of careful experiments, in which homing pigeons were fitted with small magnets, showed that this second compass was sensitive to the Earth's magnetic field. Birds without magnets returned home in the usual way; birds carrying magnets, which disturbed the local magnetic field around the head, became disoriented under overcast skies but oriented normally when the sun was visible. The magnetic compass, in other words, seems to act as a backup that takes over when celestial cues are unavailable.

Identifying a magnetic sense was relatively easy; identifying the organ responsible for it has proved much harder. Two main hypotheses are now under serious investigation. According to the first, microscopic crystals of magnetite—an iron-rich mineral—located in the upper beak respond to changes in the strength and direction of the magnetic field, and pass this information to the brain through a branch of the trigeminal nerve. According to the second, certain proteins in the retina of the eye, known as cryptochromes, undergo subtle chemical changes in the presence of a magnetic field, allowing the bird literally to "see" the field as a faint pattern superimposed on its visual surroundings. The two systems are not mutually exclusive, and recent evidence suggests that birds may use both, with the retinal system providing direction and the beak system providing information about magnetic intensity.

A compass, however, is only part of the solution. To find a particular location, an animal needs not only a sense of direction but also a sense of position—what scientists sometimes call a map. The exact nature of the bird's map remains poorly understood. Experiments with adult birds displaced thousands of kilometres from their usual route have shown that they are still able to correct their course and reach their original destination, which means they must somehow know where they are even in completely unfamiliar territory. Possible map cues include subtle variations in the strength of the magnetic field across the Earth's surface, low-frequency sound waves that travel for great distances, and characteristic odours associated with particular regions.

Young birds, on their first migration, do not yet have such a map. Instead, they appear to follow a simple genetic program that tells them roughly which direction to fly and for how long. Mistakes are common, and many young birds never reach the wintering ground at all. Those that survive learn the route in detail and refine their map over successive journeys, until, as adults, they are able to find a particular wood or marsh as reliably as a human visitor following a printed atlas. Migration, in other words, is a learned skill built on an inherited foundation, and the apparent precision of the experienced traveller is the product of long experience as well as inborn mechanisms.
