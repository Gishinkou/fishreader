Bakelite was the world's first fully synthetic plastic. Invented in 1907 by the Belgian-American chemist Leo Hendrik Baekeland, it transformed both industry and everyday life. Before Bakelite, manufacturers depended on natural materials such as ivory, horn, shellac, and rubber, all of which were expensive, scarce, or limited in their performance.

Baekeland had already made his fortune by inventing a photographic paper called Velox. He chose to invest both his money and his time in finding a substitute for shellac, a resin secreted by the lac beetle and used as an electrical insulator. After years of experimenting with phenol and formaldehyde, he discovered that under carefully controlled heat and pressure these two substances could be combined into a hard, durable, and moldable resin.

The new material possessed an unusual combination of properties. It was an excellent electrical insulator, resisted heat and chemicals, and could be molded into virtually any shape. Once set, it would not soften again, which meant it kept its form even under demanding conditions. These qualities suited it perfectly to the booming electrical industry of the early twentieth century.

Bakelite quickly found its way into telephones, radios, light fittings, and countless small mechanical parts. It also became fashionable in consumer goods such as jewelry, kitchenware, and decorative objects, prized for its glossy finish and the rich range of colors it could be dyed.

By the 1920s, Bakelite was being produced in factories around the world. Its versatility encouraged designers to experiment with new shapes and styles, contributing to the streamlined aesthetic associated with the Art Deco movement. Yet, although later plastics eventually surpassed Bakelite in some applications, the material remains in use today and is widely collected as a symbol of early twentieth-century modernity.

Baekeland's invention demonstrated that an entirely artificial substance could outperform natural materials. In doing so, it opened the door to the age of plastics and forever changed the relationship between human industry and the natural world.
