Stonehenge, on the chalk plain of Salisbury in southern England, is the most famous prehistoric monument in Europe. Although millions of people visit the site each year, few realise that the ring of standing stones they see today is only the last and most visible phase of a structure that was built, modified, and rebuilt over roughly fifteen hundred years. The earliest construction at the site dates from around 3000 BCE, and the great stones that give Stonehenge its name were raised some five centuries later. By 1600 BCE, work at the monument had effectively ceased.

The first phase of Stonehenge was not a stone circle at all but a circular earthwork: a wide ditch enclosing a flat space, with a ring of fifty-six small pits just inside the bank. Many of these pits were later found to contain cremated human remains, which has led archaeologists to suggest that the earliest function of the site was as a place of burial and ceremony for the dead. At this stage Stonehenge would have looked like one of many similar earthworks scattered across the British Isles, and there would have been little to distinguish it as the future site of one of the most ambitious building projects of the prehistoric world.

The first stones to arrive at the site were the so-called bluestones, named for the slight blue tinge of their surface when freshly broken. These stones, each weighing several tonnes, came from the Preseli Hills of western Wales, more than two hundred and forty kilometres from Salisbury Plain. How the builders moved them remains one of the great puzzles of the monument. The most widely accepted theory is that the stones were dragged on wooden sledges to the nearest navigable water, then floated on rafts along the south coast and up the rivers of Wessex before being hauled the last few kilometres overland. Recent experiments have shown that such a journey, though slow, was within the means of a determined Neolithic community.

A century or two later, the much larger sarsen stones were added. These came from the Marlborough Downs, some thirty kilometres to the north—a shorter distance, but the stones themselves are far heavier, with the largest weighing around twenty-five tonnes. They were arranged in a great outer circle of upright stones connected by horizontal lintels, with five enormous trilithons (pairs of uprights joined by a single lintel) standing inside the circle in a horseshoe shape. The careful shaping of the stones and the use of joints adapted from carpentry—projections on the uprights fitting into hollows in the lintels—show a level of engineering skill that surprised early archaeologists.

What was Stonehenge for? Theories have been almost as varied as the people who have proposed them. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, antiquarians imagined it as a temple of the Druids, although the Druids of Iron Age Britain lived more than a thousand years after the monument was completed. In the twentieth century, astronomers showed that the main axis of the structure aligns with the rising sun at the midsummer solstice and the setting sun at midwinter, prompting some scholars to argue that Stonehenge was a kind of astronomical observatory or calendar.

More recent archaeological work has shifted the emphasis from astronomy back toward burial and ritual. Excavations have shown that the site lies at the centre of a much wider sacred landscape, linked by long ceremonial avenues to other major monuments and burial mounds. Hundreds of cremation burials have been recovered, and chemical analysis of teeth from some of the buried individuals suggests that they came from across Britain—and in a few cases from continental Europe. Whatever the precise meaning of the monument, it now seems clear that for the people who built it, Stonehenge was a place where the worlds of the living and the dead met, drawing visitors from far beyond the chalk plain on which it stands.
