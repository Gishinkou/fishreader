For roughly a century, beginning in the late 1500s and continuing through most of the 1600s, the small Dutch Republic produced an extraordinary quantity of painting. By some estimates, several million pictures were painted during this period in a country whose total population never exceeded two million people. The phenomenon, now called the Dutch Golden Age, was so unusual that historians have devoted considerable effort to explaining how and why it happened in this particular place and time.

The political and religious situation of the new republic was crucial. Having won independence from Spain after a long war, the Dutch provinces adopted a Protestant form of Christianity that, unlike the Catholic Church, did not commission religious paintings for its plain whitewashed churches. The largest single source of artistic patronage in the rest of Europe—the church—was therefore closed to Dutch painters. At the same time, the absence of a powerful royal court meant that there was little of the aristocratic patronage that supported grand portraits and history paintings elsewhere. Dutch artists had to find their customers somewhere else, and they found them in the prosperous merchant class of the cities.

A successful Amsterdam merchant of the period did not commission paintings of saints or kings. He bought modest pictures to hang on the walls of his own house, and the subjects he preferred reflected the world he actually lived in: views of his city, scenes of daily life, neatly arranged tables of food and flowers, the calm flat landscape of his country, ships at sea. To meet this demand, painters specialised. One artist would devote his career to seascapes, another to church interiors, another to winter scenes with skaters on frozen canals. Specialisation allowed each painter to develop a high level of technical skill in a narrow range of subjects, and it allowed customers to seek out exactly the kind of picture they wanted.

The market was extensive. Inventories from the period show paintings hanging not only in the homes of the wealthy but also in the houses of bakers, shoemakers, and even farm labourers. Pictures were sold at annual fairs, in the workshops of the painters themselves, and through dealers who operated something close to a modern art market, with regular auctions, speculative purchases, and considerable variation in price. Most paintings were relatively inexpensive—comparable in cost to a few weeks' wages for a skilled craftsman—although the work of the most admired artists could command very high prices.

The painters of the period are best known today for their close attention to the visible world. Light falling through a window onto a polished tile floor, the texture of a starched white collar, the reflections on a pewter jug, the soft grey of a winter sky over a flat horizon—these were the subjects on which the great talents of the age, including Vermeer, Rembrandt, and Hals, lavished their skill. Even apparently casual scenes of taverns, kitchens, and courtyards were the product of careful arrangement and patient observation, and modern analysis has shown that many of them contain hidden moral messages addressed to a contemporary audience that read such pictures more attentively than we do today.

The Golden Age did not last. By the early eighteenth century, the Dutch economy had been overtaken by larger neighbours, the wealthy merchant class had grown more conservative in its tastes, and the market for cheap, freshly painted pictures had declined. Specialisation, which had once driven the system, became a trap, as painters trained in a narrow tradition found themselves unable to adapt. The pictures themselves, however, survived in remarkable numbers, and the techniques and subjects they introduced—the everyday interior, the realistic portrait, the landscape valued for its own sake—became permanent additions to the European artistic vocabulary.
