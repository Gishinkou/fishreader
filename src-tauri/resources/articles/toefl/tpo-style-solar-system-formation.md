The Sun, the planets, the moons, and the countless smaller bodies that make up the solar system all formed at roughly the same time, about four and a half billion years ago, from a single cloud of gas and dust drifting through one of the spiral arms of the Milky Way galaxy. The general outline of how this happened, known as the nebular hypothesis, was first proposed in the eighteenth century and has been refined by more than two centuries of observation and physical reasoning. It now provides a coherent explanation for many features of the solar system that would otherwise seem unrelated.

The starting material was an ordinary interstellar cloud, composed mostly of hydrogen and helium left over from the Big Bang, with smaller quantities of heavier elements produced inside earlier generations of stars. Such a cloud is normally stable, supported against its own gravity by internal pressure and slow rotation. Some external disturbance—perhaps the shock wave from a nearby supernova explosion—appears to have compressed a portion of the cloud beyond the point at which gravity could be resisted. Once contraction began, it continued under its own momentum.

As the cloud shrank, two things happened. First, its rate of rotation increased, in the same way that a spinning skater spins faster when she pulls her arms in. Second, the inward fall of material caused the central regions to heat up. The combination of rapid rotation and gravitational collapse flattened the cloud into a thin, rotating disc, with a dense central concentration that would eventually become the Sun. This stage, in which a young star is surrounded by a flat disc of leftover material, is now seen routinely in observations of star-forming regions in the galaxy, providing direct evidence that the process is general rather than peculiar to our own system.

Within the disc, dust grains and small ice particles collided gently and stuck together, building up steadily larger objects. The earliest of these, only a few millimetres across, grew into pebbles, then into boulders, then into bodies tens of kilometres in diameter, known as planetesimals. Once a planetesimal was large enough for its gravity to attract its neighbours, growth accelerated dramatically: the largest bodies swept up almost everything in their orbital path and grew into protoplanets. The smaller, leftover pieces became the asteroids and comets, fragments of an original population that never finished combining.

The composition of the planets was determined largely by their distance from the Sun. Close to the centre of the disc, temperatures were high, and only materials with very high melting points—metals and silicate rocks—could survive in solid form. The result was a small number of dense, rocky planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. Beyond a certain distance, sometimes called the snow line, temperatures were low enough for water, methane, and ammonia to freeze. The protoplanets that grew in this colder region had access to far more raw material and could become much larger. Once they had gathered enough rock and ice, their gravity was strong enough to hold on to the abundant hydrogen and helium of the disc, and they grew into the giant planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.

The end of the formation period was marked by a violent phase in which the leftover planetesimals were either swept up by the planets, ejected from the solar system altogether, or thrown into stable orbits where they remain today. The heavily cratered surfaces of the Moon, Mercury, and Mars preserve a record of this final bombardment, which appears to have ended around three and a half billion years ago. The Sun, by then, had begun to shine in essentially its present form, and the broad architecture of the solar system was settled. The smaller adjustments of the next four billion years—the migration of giant planets, the impact that produced Earth's Moon, the slow evolution of planetary atmospheres—took place against this stable background.
