Most of the volcanic islands scattered across the world's oceans owe their existence not to the edges of tectonic plates, where most of Earth's volcanism takes place, but to isolated "hot spots" within the plates themselves. A hot spot is a relatively stationary plume of unusually hot material rising from deep within the mantle. Where this plume reaches the base of an oceanic plate, it melts a portion of the overlying rock, producing magma that eventually erupts through the seafloor.

As the oceanic plate drifts slowly over the stationary plume, a chain of volcanoes is gradually constructed. The volcano sitting directly above the hot spot is actively erupting, while older volcanoes, carried away by plate motion, become extinct. Over millions of years, this process produces a long line of islands and submerged seamounts, with the youngest island at one end and progressively older, more eroded structures stretching away in the direction of plate movement. The Hawaiian Islands provide the classic example: the island of Hawaii itself is still volcanically active, while the islands to the northwest grow older and lower in elevation as one moves away from the hot spot.

The growth of such an island is a remarkable construction project. A submarine volcano must build itself up from the deep seafloor, often four or five kilometers below sea level, before it can break the surface and form dry land. Most never reach this stage at all; they remain as seamounts, hidden underwater. Those that do emerge rise quickly in geological terms—sometimes within a few hundred thousand years—because basaltic lava, the dominant material erupted at hot spots, is fluid and flows easily, allowing the volcano to spread out into broad, gently sloping shield shapes.

Once a volcano ceases to erupt, the forces of destruction take over. Waves erode the coastline, rivers cut deep valleys, and chemical weathering breaks down the rock. The island itself slowly sinks as the oceanic plate cools and contracts on its journey away from the warm hot spot. In tropical waters, coral reefs may grow upward along the shoreline as the island subsides, eventually forming a ring of reef around a shrinking central island. When the volcanic core finally disappears beneath the sea, only the coral ring remains, producing the circular atolls that dot many parts of the Pacific.

The pattern of progressively older islands stretching away from an active volcano was one of the early pieces of evidence used to confirm the theory of plate tectonics. By measuring the ages of rocks along the Hawaiian–Emperor chain, geologists were able to estimate not only the direction but also the speed of the Pacific Plate's motion over the past eighty million years. A sharp bend in the chain, where the line of islands changes direction, records a major shift in plate motion that occurred roughly forty-three million years ago, the cause of which is still debated.

Hot spots also occur beneath continents, where they can produce vast outpourings of lava known as flood basalts, but their effects are most visible in the oceans. There, isolated from the complications of continental crust, they create some of the longest, straightest, and most regular features on the planet's surface—a slow record, written in stone, of the steady drift of the plates above.
