Two species of deer have lived in the Puget Sound region of the Pacific Northwest since long before European settlers arrived. The black-tailed deer, a lowland relative of the mule deer of the drier interior, occupies the forested western slopes of the Cascade Range. The Columbian white-tailed deer, once common in the open prairies and river valleys of the lower Columbia, is now restricted to a few low, marshy islands and floodplains near the river's mouth. The two species illustrate how very different the fates of closely related animals can be when human activity reshapes a landscape.

The black-tailed deer will eat almost any plant of the forest understory. Salal, huckleberry, and the leaves of young dogwood and maple are favorites, but in winter, when these plants lose their leaves or are buried in snow, the deer turn to the foliage of conifers and to lichens that fall from the branches during storms. Black-tails do not hibernate. Instead, they migrate vertically: they spend the summer in high meadows where forage is rich and predators are few, and descend to the lowlands when the first heavy snows arrive. There the tall shrubs of the forest edge remain accessible even when the ground is white, and wind-thrown branches of cedar and hemlock provide additional browse.

The arrival of European settlers brought sudden changes. The expedition of Lewis and Clark, which reached the mouth of the Columbia River in November 1805, recorded great difficulty finding game in the heavy coastal forest. To feed forty people through the winter, the expedition killed about a hundred and fifty elk and twenty deer—a level of hunting that the local environment, with its dense vegetation and limited open ground, could not have sustained for long. Early in the spring, when game moved out of the lowlands, the expedition chose to begin its journey east rather than risk starvation.

In the decades that followed, the founding of trading posts and the rapid growth of settlements brought heavier and more sustained pressure. The Scottish botanist David Douglas, who visited Fort Vancouver several times between 1825 and 1832, noted that the deer once "scattered like cattle" across the meadows around the fort had largely disappeared, hunted to protect the post's growing fields. The Columbian white-tailed deer, dependent on these open habitats, was driven to the edge of extinction; the few remaining populations survived only in places too wet to plow.

The black-tailed deer, by contrast, has thrived under modern conditions, and in some places it is more numerous now than it was before European arrival. Three changes account for this paradox. First, the wolves, cougars, and lynx that once preyed on the deer have been greatly reduced by hunting and habitat loss. Second, state wildlife agencies have controlled human hunting through licenses, bag limits, and closed seasons. Third, and probably most important, the clearing and burning of the original forest has created vast areas of low, dense, regrowing vegetation—ideal habitat for an animal that prefers shrubs to old-growth trees.

Studies by the biologist Arthur Einarsen and others in the mid-twentieth century showed that the leaves and twigs growing in cleared land are not only more abundant than those of the deep forest but also more nutritious, with significantly higher levels of protein. The black-tailed deer, in other words, has been one of the unintended beneficiaries of logging. Its story and that of the white-tailed deer, side by side in the same region, are a reminder that the same human activities can devastate one species and bring another to unprecedented abundance.
