A honeybee colony depends on a steady supply of nectar and pollen, gathered from flowers that may be scattered over an area of many square kilometres. A foraging bee that discovers a rich patch of blossoms is therefore faced with a problem: she has, at best, a few hundred milligrams of nectar in her stomach, but a successful colony may need to bring back many kilograms of the same nectar before the flowers fade. The solution that honeybees have evolved is one of the most remarkable communication systems known in any animal—a symbolic dance, performed in the darkness of the hive, that tells other foragers exactly where to go.

The system was decoded in the middle of the twentieth century by the Austrian zoologist Karl von Frisch and his students, working at first with simple observation hives that had glass walls. Frisch noticed that a returning forager, after handing over her load of nectar, did not simply fly out again. Instead, she walked onto a vertical comb crowded with other workers and began to perform one of two distinct dances. If the food source was very close to the hive—within about a hundred metres—she performed a "round dance," moving in tight circles that alternated in direction. The other bees followed her closely, touching her with their antennae, and then flew out and searched the surrounding area at random.

For food sources further from the hive, the bee switched to a more elaborate performance now called the "waggle dance." Walking in a straight line across the comb, the dancer vibrated her abdomen rapidly from side to side, then looped back to her starting point and repeated the straight run in the opposite curve, tracing out a flattened figure of eight. Frisch's great insight was that this dance was not random. The direction of the straight run, measured against the vertical, indicated the direction of the food relative to the position of the sun. A run pointing straight up the comb meant "fly directly toward the sun"; a run forty degrees to the right of vertical meant "fly forty degrees to the right of the sun." The duration of the straight run, meanwhile, indicated the distance to the food, with longer runs corresponding to greater distances.

These claims were so unexpected that they were greeted with considerable scepticism. The decisive evidence came from a long series of experiments in which Frisch and his colleagues recorded the dances of bees that had been trained to feeding stations at known locations, and then observed where the recruited bees actually arrived. The match between the information encoded in the dance and the location to which fresh foragers flew was close enough to leave little doubt that the bees were genuinely reading the dance. Later researchers, using radar to track individual bees in flight, confirmed the result directly.

The dance is not the only channel of communication available to a foraging bee. Returning workers also give out samples of the nectar they have collected, allowing potential recruits to learn its scent, and they leave traces of an attractive odour at the food source itself. Both of these signals help recruited bees pin down the exact flowers being exploited once they have arrived in the general area indicated by the dance. The dance, in short, provides the long-range navigation, while chemical signals fine-tune the search at short range.

Recent work has continued to enrich the picture. Bees can adjust their dances to take account of obstacles such as cliffs or large bodies of water between the hive and the food, and they can even communicate the location of a possible new nest site when the colony is preparing to swarm. Each year of further research seems to reveal additional sophistication. What was once dismissed as impossible has become one of the standard examples used in biology courses to show how natural selection can produce behaviour of astonishing complexity in animals with very small brains.
