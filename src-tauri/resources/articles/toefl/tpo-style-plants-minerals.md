Plants need light, water, carbon dioxide, and a small but essential supply of mineral nutrients drawn from the soil. Although these minerals make up only a tiny fraction of the dry weight of a plant, the failure of any one of them to be present in adequate quantities can prevent the plant from completing its life cycle. Researchers therefore distinguish between major nutrients, such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, which are needed in large amounts, and micronutrients, such as iron, copper, and zinc, which are required only in trace quantities. Both categories are equally essential.

The soil from which these minerals come is itself a complex mixture of fragments of weathered rock, decaying organic matter, water, air, and living organisms. As rainwater filters through this mixture, it dissolves small amounts of soluble mineral salts. Plant roots absorb these dissolved ions along with the water in which they are dissolved. The chemical form in which a mineral is taken up matters a great deal. Nitrogen, for example, makes up most of the atmosphere as the gas N₂, but plants cannot use it in this form; they must absorb it as nitrate or ammonium ions from the soil. Bacteria living in the soil and in the roots of certain plants are responsible for converting atmospheric nitrogen into these usable forms.

When a particular mineral is in short supply, the plant develops characteristic symptoms that careful observers can use to diagnose the deficiency. A shortage of nitrogen produces general yellowing of the older leaves, because nitrogen is needed for the production of chlorophyll, the green pigment that captures sunlight. A shortage of phosphorus often produces stunted growth and an unusual reddish or purplish tinge on the underside of leaves. A shortage of iron, by contrast, affects mainly the youngest leaves, which turn pale yellow while the veins remain green. These visible signs are valuable because they appear before the plant's growth has been seriously affected, and a knowledgeable farmer or gardener can correct the problem in time.

The recycling of nutrients between living plants and the soil is normally efficient. Dead leaves, stems, and roots decay on the forest floor or are buried in the upper layers of the soil, where bacteria and fungi break them down and return their minerals to circulation. In a natural ecosystem, very little is lost. Agriculture interrupts this cycle. Every time a crop is harvested and shipped away, the minerals it contains leave the field for good. Unless they are replaced, the soil grows steadily poorer, and yields decline.

For thousands of years, farmers have tried to maintain soil fertility by spreading animal manure, plowing under crop residues, and rotating crops with nitrogen-fixing legumes such as clover and beans. In the nineteenth century, the chemical analysis of plants and soils made it possible to identify exactly which elements were being removed, and the modern fertilizer industry was born. Manufactured fertilizers, which provide nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in concentrated and predictable forms, have allowed enormous increases in agricultural production. They have also, however, created new problems: excess fertilizer washed from fields contributes to pollution of rivers and coastal waters, where the same minerals that nourish crops on land trigger explosive growth of algae offshore.

A further complication is that some soils contain too much, rather than too little, of a particular mineral. Soils with a high concentration of salt, for example, draw water out of plant roots and limit growth even when other nutrients are abundant. Researchers are now developing crop varieties that tolerate salty conditions, in the hope of using land that would otherwise be useless. The boundary between deficiency and excess, between a nutrient and a poison, is often a matter of concentration.
