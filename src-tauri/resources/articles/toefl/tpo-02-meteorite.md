Meteorites are pieces of rock and metal that have fallen to Earth from space. Most originate in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, where countless rocky bodies orbit the Sun. Occasional collisions between asteroids send fragments hurtling through the inner solar system.

When such a fragment crosses Earth's path and enters the atmosphere, friction with the air heats it intensely, creating the brilliant streak of light we call a meteor or "shooting star." Most are no larger than a grain of sand and burn up entirely before reaching the ground.

A few survive their fiery passage and land on the surface; only then are they called meteorites. Scientists classify them into three broad groups based on composition: stony meteorites, which resemble ordinary rocks on Earth; iron meteorites, composed mostly of iron and nickel; and stony-iron meteorites, which contain a mixture of both.

Stony meteorites are by far the most common. Among them, the chondrites are particularly valuable to researchers because they contain small spherical grains called chondrules that formed very early in the history of the solar system. Studying these grains gives scientists a window into the conditions that existed more than 4.5 billion years ago.

Rarer still are meteorites that originated not from asteroids but from the Moon or Mars. These were blasted off their parent bodies by large impacts, drifted through space for millions of years, and eventually fell to Earth. Although only a few hundred such meteorites are known, they provide priceless samples of other worlds for laboratories on Earth.

Despite the popular image of meteor strikes as catastrophic events, the vast majority of meteorites are small and harmless. They are, however, treasured by scientists as natural messengers from the distant past of our solar system.
