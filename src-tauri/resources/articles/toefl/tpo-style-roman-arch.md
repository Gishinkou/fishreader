The arch is one of the most important structural inventions in the history of building. It was not invented by the Romans—simple arches were used in the underground drains and tombs of earlier Mediterranean cultures—but it was the Romans who first understood its possibilities on a large scale and used it as a routine element in everyday construction. From the arch they derived the vault, which is essentially a continuous arch extended in depth, and the dome, which can be thought of as an arch rotated about a vertical axis. These three forms gave Roman architects the ability to roof spaces far larger than anything that could be spanned by a wooden beam or a flat stone lintel.

The basic principle of the arch is simple. A horizontal beam carries its load by resisting bending, and stone is a poor material for that purpose: it is strong in compression but weak in tension. A beam of stone supported only at the ends will crack along its underside long before it is crushed. An arch, by contrast, is made of wedge-shaped blocks called voussoirs, fitted together along a curve. The load pressing down on the arch is transmitted from voussoir to voussoir as compression, eventually reaching the vertical supports at either side. Because stone is extremely strong in compression, an arch built of small blocks can carry far greater weights over much greater spans than a horizontal stone beam ever could.

The penalty for this strength is that an arch pushes outward as well as downward. The two supports at its base must therefore resist a horizontal thrust, and this requirement governs much of Roman architectural design. A single, isolated arch needs heavy buttresses to hold its supports in place. But when arches are arranged in a row, the outward thrust of one arch is balanced by the opposing thrust of its neighbor, so that only the outermost supports need to be massive. The Roman aqueducts that still stride across the countryside of southern France and Spain take advantage of exactly this principle, repeating the same arch over and over for hundreds of meters.

The vault, formed by extending an arch lengthwise, allowed the Romans to roof long halls without any internal columns. The simplest form, called a barrel vault, looks like a tunnel of stone or concrete. Two barrel vaults crossing at right angles produce a groin vault, which concentrates its weight on four corner piers rather than along two continuous walls; this made it possible to open the side walls with large windows, transforming the interior into a bright, airy space. Roman engineers used groin vaults extensively in the great public baths, where vast halls had to be both well lit and free of obstructing supports.

The dome is the arch's most spectacular descendant. In a dome, the same principle of compression operates in every direction around a central axis, so that the weight of the structure is distributed evenly into a circular base. The Pantheon in Rome, completed in the early second century, is the supreme example of Roman use of the dome. Its concrete shell, more than forty meters across, was the largest dome in the world for thirteen hundred years, and it remains the largest unreinforced concrete dome ever built. The Romans achieved this feat partly by lightening the concrete with progressively less dense aggregates—heavy basalt at the bottom, light volcanic pumice near the top—so that the dome grows lighter as it rises.

Without the arch, the great public buildings of Rome—the basilicas, the baths, the aqueducts, the amphitheaters—would have been impossible. By repeating, combining, and elaborating this single geometric idea, Roman builders solved problems of scale that would not be matched again until the development of iron and steel construction in the nineteenth century.
